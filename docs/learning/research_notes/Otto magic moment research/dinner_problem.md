# The "what's for dinner" problem: size and pain for home cooks

Status: draft · Research notes, not code facts · Compiled 2026-11-04 from web searches (search-result summaries; primary PDFs were not opened unless stated)

Labels used below. **[VENDOR]** means the survey was paid for by a company that sells a dinner solution, so it has an interest in the result being large. **[GOV]** means a government or UN source. **[ACAD]** means peer-reviewed or academic. **[MEDIA/BLOG]** means secondary and weak.

---

## 1. Decision fatigue: how often people decide, how stressful it is

### Takeaway
Deciding what to eat is the pain people name most often, but the strongest numbers come from vendor surveys. The best one is Factor/Wakefield (Dec 2024, couples only): 68% call deciding what to eat their biggest meal-time challenge. No neutral (Pew/Gallup/YouGov) survey turned up that measures "what's for dinner" stress directly.

### Cited Findings
- **[VENDOR: Factor, a meal-delivery brand owned by HelloFresh; fieldwork by Wakefield Research, Dec 10–16 2024, n=1,000 US adults who are married or living as married]**: 68% say deciding what to eat is their biggest meal-time challenge. 44% of couples struggle to settle on a plan most nights. 25% of couples argue about dinner once or twice a week. 46% would give up social media forever if they never had to plan dinner again. — [Business Wire, Factor release, Jan 27 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250127681988/en); [Gourmet News summary](https://www.gourmetnews.com/factor/)
- The sample covers couples only, so these are not estimates for all adults. — same source
- **[VENDOR: Hopscotch; fieldwork by OnePoll]**: more than half of US adults make the same meals week after week, rising to 65% of Gen Z. An AP feature calls this "dinner fatigue": the mental tiredness of deciding what to cook. — [AP via Washington Times, Sep 29 2026](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/sep/29/dinner-fatigue-fix-planning/)
- Words people use: "dinner fatigue", "the hassle of meal planning", "too tired to cook", "what's for dinner", "never having to plan dinner again". — sources above and in section 2

### Inferences
- People decide dinner about 7 times a week, every week. That frequency is the strongest argument that this pain is large. It is inferred from how daily life works, not measured by a source.
- The 46% "give up social media" figure is the most quoted number, and it comes from a vendor's hypothetical question. Use it as an example of the language people use, not as a measure of how big the problem is.

### Gaps
- No neutral survey found on how much time people spend deciding dinner (minutes per day). Widely shared "minutes spent deciding" figures could not be traced to a primary source.
- No Pew, Gallup or YouGov measure found of stress about "what's for dinner".

## 2. Time: time available on weeknights, and meal-prep adoption

### Takeaway
The average American adult spends about 40 minutes a day on food preparation and cleanup. Women average about 52 minutes and men about 28. "Too tired to cook after work" is a common feeling.

### Cited Findings
- **[GOV: BLS American Time Use Survey 2024]**: food preparation and cleanup averages 0.67 h/day across the whole population (about 40 min). Women average about 0.86 h (about 52 min) and men about 0.46 h (about 28 min). These averages include people who did no cooking that day. — [BLS ATUS charts](https://www.bls.gov/charts/american-time-use/activity-by-hldh.htm); [BLS table A-1 2024](https://www.bls.gov/tus/tables/a1-2024.pdf)
- **[ACAD, from ATUS 2003–2023]**: among people who cook on a given day, men's time rose from 45 to 50 min/day while women's stayed at 71 min. Men's participation rose until about 2021 and then levelled off. — [News-Medical summary, Sep 2025](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20250905/Men-close-the-cooking-gap-as-home-meal-prep-rises-across-the-US.aspx)
- **[VENDOR: Kevin's Natural Foods; fieldwork by Talker Research, 2024]**: about 8 in 10 Americans say they are too tired to cook a meal after work. — [WTVY, Aug 31 2024](https://www.wtvy.com/2024/08/31/nearly-80-americans-are-too-tired-cook-after-work-research-says)
- **[Agency study: HUNTER Food Study, Dec 2020]**: 51% were cooking more during the pandemic and 81% said they enjoy cooking. A later HUNTER wave found fewer people saying they "love" cooking (19%, down from 22%). — [FoodNavigator-USA, Jan 2021](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2021/01/15/Survey-Americans-committed-to-maintaining-cooking-habits-in-post-pandemic-world/); [Tasting Table](https://tastingtable.com/918429/new-research-shows-people-are-over-this-pandemic-food-trend)

### Inferences
- If the average cook spends 50–70 minutes a day on food, a 45–60 minute recipe plus shopping uses up most of that time. Weeknight recipes probably need to take 30 minutes or less. This is inferred; the sources did not measure recipe times.

### Gaps
- No reliable data found on the share of adults who meal-prep in batches.
- No source found that compares the time recipes take with the time people have.
- No 2024–2026 HUNTER Food Study figures found.

## 3. Money: grocery inflation, takeout and delivery spend, planning to save

### Takeaway
Food prices rose sharply. Overall food prices were up about 10% in 2022, then rose about 2.5–3% a year. Takeout and delivery cost households roughly $130–150 a month, and nearly everyone believes cooking at home saves money. Money is the motivation people already act on.

### Cited Findings
- **[GOV: USDA ERS, as reported]**: food prices rose by up to about 10% in 2022 (after about 4% in 2021), about 2.5% in 2024 and about 3% in 2025. Forecasts for 2026 vary by release: food at home +1.7% in February, then +2.5% in a later release, while food away from home rises faster (+3.7–4.6%). — [FoodNavigator-USA, Feb 24 2026](https://foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2026/02/24/2026-food-price-outlook-grocery-inflation-slows-dining-costs-up); [RFD-TV](https://www.rfdtv.com/usda-food-price-outlook-forecasts-3-point-1-percent-rise-in-2026-beef-eggs-grocery-trends?p=96); [Grocery Dive](https://www.grocerydive.com/news/food-at-home-prices-increase-2026-usda/813359/)
- **[ACAD: Purdue Consumer Food Insights, Sep 2024]**: food spending is about $83 per person per week, up 15% since January 2022. — [Purdue CFDAS report](https://ag.purdue.edu/cfdas/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Report_202409-2.pdf)
- **[VENDOR: Flashfood, a grocery app; fieldwork by Harris Poll, Dec 17–19 2024, n=2,089]**: 89% say cooking at home is one of the best ways to save money on food and is healthier than takeout. 83% say saving money is a bigger priority in 2025. — [Business Wire via Observer-Reporter, Jan 14 2025](https://stocks.observer-reporter.com/observerreporter/article/bizwire-2025-1-14-survey-reveals-majority-of-americans-plan-to-cook-at-home-more-in-2025-to-save-money)
- **[Media survey: LendingTree, n>2,000]**: 88% have changed how they spend on groceries. 61% felt afraid they could not afford groceries in the past month. 59% eat out less because of inflation. — [Mahoning Matters](https://www.mahoningmatters.com/news/nation-world/article300975164.html)
- **Delivery spend [VENDOR/media surveys]**:
  - Circuit survey (n=1,021): $33.89 per order, $152.51 a month, $1,843.72 a year, of which $12.80 per order goes on fees and tips. — [Circuit](https://getcircuit.com/teams/blog/hidden-cost-of-delivery)
  - Upgraded Points (2023, n≈2,100): about $1,566 a year, 3.7 orders a month. — [Upgraded Points](https://upgradedpoints.com/news/americans-food-delivery-habits)
  - LendingTree (2023): $407 a month among delivery customers. This is an outlier, probably because it surveys a different group. — [Nasdaq/LendingTree coverage](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/the-average-american-spends-this-much-per-week-on-food-delivery)
  - FinanceBuzz (2026): Gen Z places 4.3 delivery orders a month and Boomers 1.5. — [FinanceBuzz/GOBankingRates coverage](https://www.gobankingrates.com/?p=2999495)

### Inferences
- Money is a "hair on fire" pain, and people already spend on it: they pay about $150 a month for delivery that they themselves see as wasteful. An app that turns "I'm tired, order in" into "a 20-minute dinner from what I have" goes after spending that already exists.
- Restaurant prices are rising faster than grocery prices, which makes cooking at home relatively cheaper over 2025–2026.

### Gaps
- No neutral national figure found for the share of people who plan meals to save money.
- No primary source found that compares the cost of a meal cooked at home with the same meal from takeout. A "delivery costs up to 91% more" claim could not be traced and is excluded.

## 4. Food waste

### Takeaway
Households throw away about 79 kg of food per person each year (global average) and about $728 per person in the US. WRAP finds that much of this waste comes from things planning can fix: buying the wrong amount, cooking too much, food not used in time, and planned meals that don't happen.

### Cited Findings
- **[GOV/UN: UNEP Food Waste Index Report 2024, 2022 data]**: households waste 79 kg per person per year, out of 132 kg across all sectors. That is about 631 Mt, 60% of all food waste. Household waste per person differs by only about 7 kg between high-income and lower-middle-income countries. — [UNEP press release](https://unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/world-squanders-over-1-billion-meals-day-un-report); [Key messages PDF](https://wedocs.unep.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.11822/45275/Food-Waste-Index-2024-key-messages.pdf?sequence=4)
- **[GOV: US EPA, cost report released April 2025]**: uneaten food costs about $728 per person per year, about $2,913 for a family of four, or about $56 a week. This covers home and food service and uses retail prices. It replaces the older figure of $1,500 per household. — [EPA, Estimating the Cost of Food Waste to American Consumers](https://epa.gov/land-research/estimating-cost-food-waste-american-consumers)
- US pounds per person: the numbers conflict. One source cites 549 lb per person disposed (EPA 2021, all sectors) and another cites 349 lb for households, but neither was verified against an EPA primary page. ReFED puts wasted food at more than $760 per person in 2024. — [secondary summaries via search; not verified]
- **[GOV-funded: WRAP UK]**: UK household food waste is about 6.0 Mt a year (2022), down 9% from 2021 and 22% since 2007. The most common reasons are too much cooked, prepared or served, then personal preference, then food that went off or passed its date. WRAP estimates nearly 40% of household food waste happens because food was not used in time. — [WRAP household food waste study](https://data.wrap.ngo/story/the-household-food-waste-study); [Circular Online](https://www.circularonline.co.uk/news/household-food-waste-threatens-uk-net-zero-target-wrap-says/)
- **[GOV-funded: WRAP Household Food Management Survey, fieldwork Jun–Jul 2024, report Mar 2025]**: the main drivers are struggling to judge and buy the right amount, and frequent "displaced meals", meaning planned meals that aren't eaten as intended. 64% think some household food waste is "inevitable". — [WRAP report](https://wrap.ngo/resources/report/understanding-household-food-waste-uk-household-food-management-survey); [Circular Online](https://www.circularonline.co.uk/news/64-of-people-think-some-household-food-waste-is-inevitable/)

### Inferences
- WRAP's "displaced meals" finding cuts both ways. Rigid weekly plans that fall apart can cause waste. A flexible plan that adapts to what's in the fridge fits the evidence better than a fixed 7-day plan.
- Most people do not feel food waste as an urgent personal pain (64% think some is inevitable). It works better as a secondary benefit that comes with saving money.

### Gaps
- No US figure found for the share of household waste caused specifically by not planning or by forgetting what's in the fridge.

## 5. Health and diet, mixed-diet households, families with kids

### Takeaway
Most Americans now follow some named eating pattern: 57% in 2025, up from 36% in 2018. The best academic link between meal planning and better diet (Ducrot 2017) is correlational, not causal. Parents' main dinner constraints are picky kids and cooking more than one meal.

### Cited Findings
- **[ACAD: Ducrot et al. 2017, IJBNPA, NutriNet-Santé, n=40,554 French adults, cross-sectional]**: 57% planned meals at least occasionally. Planners were more likely to follow nutritional guidelines and ate a wider variety of foods. Among women, planning was linked to lower odds of overweight (OR 0.92) and obesity (OR 0.79). Among men, only obesity was linked (OR 0.81). The authors say no causal conclusion can be drawn. — [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5288891/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28153017/)
- **[Industry-funded nonprofit: IFIC Food & Health Survey]**: 57% followed a specific diet or eating pattern in the past year in 2025, compared with 54% in 2024 and 36% in 2018. High-protein is the top pattern (23% in 2025, 20% in 2024). — [FoodNavigator, Jan 2026](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/01/21/ific-survey-how-americans-view-health-and-food-choices/); [IFIC Spotlight PDF](https://ific.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/IFIC-Spotlight-Survey-Healthy-Eating-in-America.pdf)
- **[VENDOR: HelloFresh UK, 2019]**: 45% of UK parents cook more than one meal a night to please fussy eaters, and two-thirds stick to a regular menu of meals they know will be eaten. — [HelloFresh UK blog](https://blog.hellofresh.co.uk/cooking-for-fussy-eaters/)
- **[VENDOR: HelloFresh NZ, 2019]**: 79% of parents cook the same meals because the kids will eat them, and 24% of households rotate only 3–4 meals a week. This is older than the 2020 cutoff and from New Zealand, so it is an outside comparison only. — [Scoop NZ](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/AK1907/S00319/kiwi-parents-forced-to-cook-multiple-dinners.htm)

### Inferences
- Mixed diets inside one household are common now that about 57% follow some pattern: one partner on high-protein, a picky child, someone with an allergy. Working out one dinner that suits everyone is a real constraint problem that apps handle poorly. This is inferred and not measured directly.

### Gaps
- No reliable 2020–2026 figure found on how many US households have members with different diets or allergies.
- No US-specific 2020–2026 neutral survey found on parents' dinner stress.

## 6. The "saved recipe graveyard"

### Takeaway
Everyone recognises it, but almost nobody has measured it. No survey was found that gives the share of saved recipes that never get cooked.

### Cited Findings
- **[Qualitative research firm, FishDog/Ditto, small sample]**: all participants admitted to saving far more recipes than they cook. Whether content fits a weeknight is what turns a save into a cooked meal. — [FishDog study](https://fishdog-web.pages.dev/research-studies/cooking-content-weeknight-applicability-drives-conversion)
- **[Pollfish survey, as reported]**: only 31% almost always make a viral recipe more than once. — [via search summary; primary not opened]
- Social media is now a main source of recipe ideas, ahead of cookbooks. — [Fox News survey coverage](https://www.foxnews.com/food-drink/cooking-survey-says-americans-prefer-to-find-recipes-on-social-media-rather-than-cookbooks); [PR Newswire "Kitchen intimidation" report](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/kitchen-intimidation-no-more--new-report-reveals-more-than-half-of-americans-credit-social-media-for-their-kitchen-confidence-301755669.html)
- Anecdote, not data: "99% of the recipes I have saved on social media I've never cooked." — [Reddit snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1syey7w/snapshots/2026-04-29T10%3A15%3A09.860593Z)

### Inferences
- The gap between saving and cooking is a good story for a product but has no data behind it. Otto could measure it itself (share of saved recipes cooked within 30 days) and own the statistic.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study found on the save-to-cook ratio on Instagram, TikTok or Pinterest. A George Mason University study is mentioned in the press, but its numbers were not found.

## 7. Household coordination and mental load

### Takeaway
The invisible work of noticing that dinner is needed, planning it and keeping track of it falls mostly on women, even when partners share the cooking. Carrying this mental load is linked to stress and burnout.

### Cited Findings
- **[ACAD: Daminger 2019, American Sociological Review; book *What's on Her Mind*, Princeton 2025, more than 170 interviews]**: Daminger splits cognitive labour into four steps: anticipate, identify, decide, monitor. Women do most of the anticipating and monitoring, while deciding is more often shared. Women who handed cooking to their husbands still had to remind them about ingredients and timing. — [WPR / To The Best of Our Knowledge](https://www.wpr.org/news/women-carry-households-mental-load-wisconsin-researcher); [UW–Madison L&S](https://ls.wisc.edu/news/how-invisible-labor-affects-relationships); [Harvard Radcliffe](https://www.radcliffe.harvard.edu/news-and-ideas/the-unseen-inequity-of-cognitive-labor)
- **[ACAD: USC study, 322 mothers of young children]**: a heavier cognitive load, with meal planning among the examples, was linked to more depression, stress and burnout. — [USC Public Exchange](https://publicexchange.usc.edu/?p=4938)
- **[GOV: ATUS 2024]**: women spend about 1.9 times as much time on food preparation as men (52 vs 28 min/day). — [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/charts/american-time-use/activity-by-hldh.htm)
- **[Pew 2019, outside the date window]**: in US couples, women do more of the cooking and grocery shopping. — [Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/09/24/among-u-s-couples-women-do-more-cooking-and-grocery-shopping-than-men/)
- **[VENDOR: Factor 2024]**: 25% of couples argue about dinner once or twice a week. — [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250127681988/en)

### Inferences
- A shared plan or list can move the "monitor" and "anticipate" steps out of one person's head into a system both partners can see. That matches Daminger's framework, but no study has shown that an app actually redistributes the load.

### Gaps
- No data found on how widely shared grocery lists or meal plans are used (for example AnyList or Apple Reminders shared lists).

## 8. Motivation and joy

### Takeaway
Most people say they enjoy cooking (81% in Dec 2020), but enthusiasm faded after the pandemic. Social media is credited with building cooking confidence. The data on joy is thin and mostly from vendors or PR.

### Cited Findings
- **[Agency: HUNTER, Dec 2020]**: 81% enjoy cooking. A later wave found fewer saying they "love" cooking (19%, down from 22%) or "like" it (22%, down from 24%). — [FoodNavigator-USA](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2021/01/15/Survey-Americans-committed-to-maintaining-cooking-habits-in-post-pandemic-world/); [Tasting Table](https://tastingtable.com/918429/new-research-shows-people-are-over-this-pandemic-food-trend)
- **[PR survey]**: more than half of Americans credit social media for their confidence in the kitchen ("kitchen intimidation no more"). — [PR Newswire](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/kitchen-intimidation-no-more--new-report-reveals-more-than-half-of-americans-credit-social-media-for-their-kitchen-confidence-301755669.html)
- **[Media survey, 2023]**: 75% fear missing out on social-media food trends. — [TheGrio](https://thegrio.com/2023/05/15/lobster-mac-n-cheese-bang-bang-shrimp-75-of-people-fear-missing-out-on-social-media-food-trends-survey-finds)

### Gaps
- No neutral data found on cooking as self-care, or on beginner cooks' confidence measured over time.

## 9. Synthesis: urgent vs nice to have, segments, current alternatives

### Takeaway
The urgent pains are the nightly decision combined with being too tired at 6pm, and money (delivery spend, grocery inflation). Both happen often, feel intense, and people already pay to fix them: about $150 a month on delivery, plus meal kits. Food waste, health, the recipe graveyard and joy are real but secondary: people value them, but they don't drive daily behaviour or spending on their own.

### Cited Findings (evidence that people already pay)
- Delivery costs about $1,566–1,844 a year per user ([Upgraded Points](https://upgradedpoints.com/news/americans-food-delivery-habits); [Circuit](https://getcircuit.com/teams/blog/hidden-cost-of-delivery)).
- Meal kits fill the same need but struggle to keep customers. HelloFresh active customers fell from 7.1M to 6.6M in 2023 and from about 7.3M to about 6.9M in 2025, and orders fell 12% in 2025. HelloFresh names meal quality and menu breadth as what keeps customers. — [RetailDetail](https://www.retaildetail.eu/news/food/hellofresh-earns-more-from-fewer-customers); [HelloFresh 2025 annual report via financialreports.eu](https://financialreports.eu/filings/hellofresh-se/management-reports/2026/32970107/); [Reuters via TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com%2C2025%3Anewsml_L8N3PU0AJ%3A0-hellofresh-drops-10-on-poor-2025-outlook)
- What people do instead: repeat the same meals (more than half of adults, 65% of Gen Z, [AP/Hopscotch-OnePoll VENDOR](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/sep/29/dinner-fatigue-fix-planning/)), order delivery, use meal kits, and save recipes from social media ([FishDog](https://fishdog-web.pages.dev/research-studies/cooking-content-weeknight-applicability-drives-conversion)).

### Inferences
- **Pains ranked by how frequent, how intense, and whether people already pay**:

  | Pain | Rating | Why |
  |---|---|---|
  | Nightly decision + tiredness | Urgent | Every day, high intensity, people pay |
  | Money | Urgent | Weekly, high intensity, people pay |
  | Mental load and coordination | High for couples and parents | Intense, but nobody pays for it directly |
  | Picky kids and mixed diets | High for families | |
  | Health goals | Medium | Wanted, but people don't act on it daily |
  | Food waste | Low to medium | Felt mostly as money; 64% see it as "inevitable" |
  | Saved-recipe graveyard | Low | Resonates, but no evidence anyone pays for it |
  | Joy and confidence | Low urgency | Good for retention, not for getting people to start |

- **Segments, by how strongly they feel the pain (inferred from the evidence above)**:
  - **Parents with kids**: picky eaters, more than one meal a night, mental load, the most dinners to plan.
  - **Couples, especially young ones**: the Factor data shows nightly negotiation and arguments; Gen Z also orders the most delivery.
  - **Health-focused people**: 57% follow a named eating pattern.
  - **Singles and students**: feel the money and waste pain from cooking for one, but less of the coordination pain.
- **Implication for a meal-planning app**: the evidence supports solving the decision at 5–6pm in seconds, cheaply, with what's already in the fridge. A fixed weekly plan is less well supported. WRAP's "displaced meals" finding and meal kits losing customers both suggest that rigid plans fail.

### Gaps
- Few neutral surveys exist. Most quantified "dinner stress" numbers come from Factor, HelloFresh, Hopscotch, Kevin's or Flashfood. A neutral survey (YouGov, Pew) or Otto's own user research would make this stronger.
- No data found on paid adoption or retention of meal-planning apps (Mealime, Paprika, Plan to Eat) to compare against.
