# Magic moment / aha moment / activation: theory and practice

Research notes, 2026-11-04. Method note: primary-source pages for lennysnewsletter.com, blog.superhuman.com and amplitude.com could not be fetched from this session (those hosts are blocked by the session network). Findings below come from web search results and the secondary pages they surfaced. Each claim is labelled **[documented]** (company or author's own account, or a peer-reviewed paper), **[secondary]** (reported by others, consistent across sources), or **[folklore]** (repeated widely, no primary source found).

## Definitions and distinctions (aha vs. magic moment vs. activation; setup → aha → habit; time-to-value; North Star vs. activation metric)

### Takeaway
"Aha moment" (the moment a user first feels the core value) and "activation milestone" (a measurable event that predicts long-term retention) are used interchangeably in practice, but they are different things: the aha is a feeling, and the activation event is a measurable stand-in for it. The setup → aha → habit sequence is a common analytics convention rather than a canonical Chamath framework. Chamath's own framing was three questions: get people in the door, get them to the aha quickly, deliver core value often.

### Cited Findings
- Lenny Rachitsky's working definition: "Your activation milestone (often referred to as your 'aha moment') is the earliest point in your onboarding flow that, by showing your product's value, is predictive of long-term retention." [documented: Lenny's own text, via search snippet] — [Lenny's Newsletter, "What is a good activation rate"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)
- Amplitude defines the aha moment as the first time a user understands the product's core value, and warns that "a smooth onboarding experience is important, but it's not the aha moment itself." [documented, Amplitude blog] — [Amplitude, "The 'Aha' Moment"](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/aha-moment)
- The four-event model "signed up → setup moment → aha moment → habit moment" appears in Amplitude's community and in course material. Setup moment: the user has done what is needed to be able to reach the aha. Habit moment: the user has hit the aha often enough to form a routine. [secondary; the exact definitions vary by source] — [Amplitude Community, "The 4 stages of activation"](https://community.amplitude.com/discussion/12791/the-4-stages-of-activation); [Platzi course: setup moment](https://platzi.com/cursos/activacion-y-engagement/definicion-y-estrategias-del-setup-momen/); [Platzi: habit moment](https://platzi.com/cursos/activacion-y-engagement/definiendo-y-analizando-tu-habit-moment/)
- A PLG guide defines the habit moment as the point where a user does the core behaviour at the product's *natural frequency* (daily, weekly and so on). [secondary] — [skills.sh activation-metrics](https://skills.sh/skenetechnologies/plg-skills/activation-metrics)
- Chamath Palihapitiya's Facebook growth talk set three questions: how to get people in the door, how to get them to the "aha moment" as quickly as possible, and how to deliver core product value as often as possible. He reportedly banned his team from talking about "virality." [secondary: talk digests] — [Recapio digest of Chamath / Gagan Biyani talk](https://recapio.com/digest/chamath-palihapitiya-how-we-put-facebook-on-the-path-to-1-billion-users-by-gagan-biyani); [Airtribe, Facebook growth lessons](https://airtribe.live/blog/product-growth-lessons-from-facebook)
- Search found no source that attributes the words "setup moment" or "habit moment" to Chamath. [negative finding] — [search results for Chamath + setup/habit moment](https://recapio.com/digest/chamath-palihapitiya-how-we-put-facebook-on-the-path-to-1-billion-users-by-gagan-biyani)
- Ben Williams (Snyk) warns that activation is often called the "aha moment", but focusing only on that can be a trap. In his view activation is a staged journey, not one event. [secondary, via Lenny] — [Lenny's Newsletter activation post](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/i/77646597/what-are-the-most-common-ways-to-increase-activation)

### Inferences
- A workable taxonomy for a founder: **value proposition** (the promise) → **setup moment** (the minimum input) → **aha / magic moment** (the felt payoff, described in words) → **activation event** (a measurable proxy for the aha, with a threshold and a time window, such as "X within N days") → **habit moment** (the core action repeated at the natural frequency) → **North Star** (an ongoing measure of value delivered across all users). The activation metric is a leading indicator for the North Star. It is not the same metric.
- "Magic moment" is mostly a synonym for aha, used more often for the emotional, felt experience. "Time-to-value" is the time from first open to the aha. "First value" is the first moment any value is delivered, even before the full aha.

### Gaps
- North Star metric definitions (Amplitude North Star Playbook, Sean Ellis) were not fetched, so no citation is given. Commonly stated distinction: the North Star captures value delivered at steady state, and the activation metric predicts whether a new user will get there.
- Could not fetch Reforge's own definitions of setup/aha/habit. Reforge (Brian Balfour, Casey Winters) is widely credited with popularising the three-moment model, but this is unverified here.

## Famous activation metrics: how they were found, documented vs. folklore

### Takeaway
Only a few of the famous numbers trace back to an insider's own words, and even those were *correlations* found in retention data and then used as onboarding targets. Facebook's "7 friends in 10 days" and Twitter's "follow ~30" come from insider talks but are known mainly through secondary retellings. Slack's "2,000 messages" is attributed to a 2015 Butterfield interview, but no primary text was found. Duolingo's streak work is the best documented (CPO's own write-up). Dropbox, Zoom, Pinterest and Airbnb numbers could not be verified in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- **Facebook, "7 friends in 10 days."** Credited to Chamath's growth team, and described as a strong *correlation* between reaching 7 friends in 10 days and long-term retention. [secondary; widely repeated; some retellings invert it to "10 friends in 7 days"] — [Recapio digest](https://recapio.com/digest/chamath-palihapitiya-how-we-put-facebook-on-the-path-to-1-billion-users-by-gagan-biyani); [Airtribe substack (inverted version)](https://airtribe.substack.com/p/pm-growth-lessons-for-2024); [PM case study noting it's a proxy, not a cause](https://scratch-puffin-363.notion.site/Facebook-s-7-friends-activation-story-a-PM-case-study-33dec508b5118053bff8e0233fedd0f3)
- One commentator argues the "7 friends" metric is now dead or misapplied as a template. [opinion] — [Caroline Frost, "Facebook's 7 friends metric is dead"](https://carolinefrost.github.io/2021/11/16/facebooks-7-friends-metric-is-dead)
- Andrew Chen's *The Cold Start Problem* retells the Facebook and Slack thresholds as examples of the "magic number" at which a network becomes valuable to a user. [secondary: reader notes] — [Goodreads notes on The Cold Start Problem](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/56962799-the-cold-start-problem/10957646-claus-enevoldsen/8fa37a22-ebf6-4a8b-85cc-88df22124721); [Medium summary](https://medium.com/twosapp/the-cold-start-problem-how-to-start-and-scale-network-effects-by-andrew-chen-813f0668c70f)
- **Twitter, "follow ~30."** Josh Elman (ex-Twitter growth) reported that users who followed about 30 accounts, with roughly a third of them following back, became active long term. Twitter then rebuilt the first-time experience to get new users to 30 follows quickly, with interest-based suggestions. An earlier version of the finding was "5–10 manually chosen follows on day one." [secondary: insider talks, retold] — [Mattermark: how Josh Elman identified a growth driver](https://mattermark.com/putting-it-all-together-how-josh-elman-identified-a-growth-driver-at-twitter/); [Medium, "Reading Josh Elman on growth"](https://medium.com/growth-hacker/reading-josh-elman-on-growth-6d379a6c153d); [Anuradha Sridharan, "The engagement challenge"](https://www.anuradhasridharan.com/2014/04/the-engagement-challenge.html)
- **Slack, "2,000 messages."** Attributed to Butterfield in a 2015 Recode interview: teams that sent about 2,000 messages almost always stayed or converted. No primary transcript was found. [folklore-to-secondary; verify against Recode before quoting] — [Frederick.ai founder story](https://frederick.ai/blog/stewart-butterfield-slack); [VentureBeat on Slack's early growth](https://venturebeat.com/business/why-is-messaging-app-slack-exploding-founder-i-have-no-f-king-idea)
- Butterfield quote collections include the line that you have to work out what conversion, retention and activation mean for your own product. [secondary] — [Quotestats](https://quotestats.com/author/stewart-butterfield-quotes/)
- **Duolingo, streaks.** CPO Jorge Mazal's own account: by mid-2018 DAU growth had slowed to single digits year over year. A counter-style gamification mechanic did not move retention. A referral program added only 3% more new users. Users who reached a 10-day streak were much less likely to drop off. The first big win was a "streak saver" notification, followed by calendar views, animations, streak-freeze changes and streak rewards. The share of DAU with a streak of 7+ days rose almost 3x, and DAU grew 4.5x over four years. These gains came from many combined efforts, not from streaks alone. [documented: insider's own write-up, numbers not independently audited] — [Lenny's Newsletter, "How Duolingo reignited user growth" (Mazal)](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth); [streak vector section](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/i/104096876/the-streak-vector)
- Lenny's activation benchmark survey of 500+ products: an average activation rate of about 34%, and 36% for SaaS. A different secondary source puts the SaaS median nearer 30%. [secondary summaries disagree; original tables not fetched] — [eChai summary](https://echai.ventures/gtm/r/what-is-a-good-activation-rate--r-plg-7); [Userpilot citing 36%](https://userpilot.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/196703); [Lenny's Newsletter original](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)

### Inferences
- Every famous number has the same shape: a core action × a threshold × a time window, found by looking backwards at retained users. Each was used as an onboarding *target* (Facebook's friend suggestions, Twitter's follow suggestions), not as proof of causation.
- For a consumer habit app, Duolingo is the closest documented analogue: the key metric was not a one-off first action but *repetition* (a streak), backed by a re-engagement prompt.

### Gaps
- **Dropbox** "one file in one folder on one device", **Zoom**, **Pinterest** and **Airbnb** activation metrics: no source was found in this pass. Treat them as [folklore] until verified. (Airbnb's professional-photography story is well known, but it concerns supply quality, not an activation metric.)
- Slack's 2,000-message claim needs the original Recode/Kara Swisher 2015 interview to move it to [documented].

## Methods to find a magic moment from data and interviews

### Takeaway
The standard method has three steps: (1) brainstorm candidate milestones, (2) test which ones best separate retained from churned users, using cohort retention and correlation or regression, then (3) *prove causation with an experiment* that pushes more users through the milestone. Pair this with qualitative work: JTBD interviews to learn why, and the Sean Ellis "very disappointed" survey to learn whether the product works for anyone at all. Superhuman's engine turns the Ellis survey into a roadmap.

### Cited Findings
- Lenny's guide to choosing an activation metric: brainstorm milestones → regress them against retention → run experiments to prove causation. [secondary summary of Lenny] — [Personal Learnings mirror: "How to determine your activation metric"](https://personal-learnings.pages.dev/reading_room/issues/lennys_newsletter/0440_how-to-determine-your-activation-metric.html)
- Amplitude's product analytics is built around behaviour → retention correlation. Its "milestone"/aha analysis tools are compared with alternatives such as Stormly. [vendor/secondary] — [Stormly, Amplitude milestone analysis vs. Stormly](https://www.stormly.com/blog/aha-moment-analytics-amplitude-vs-stormly); [Amplitude, six aha moments](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/six-aha-moments)
- **Sean Ellis test.** The question is "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?" (very / somewhat / not disappointed). It came from Ellis's work at Xobni. He set the 40% "very disappointed" benchmark after comparing nearly a hundred startups: companies that struggled to grow were almost always under 40%. At Lookout an early score was 7%. [secondary, consistent] — [CRV, PMF survey](https://www.crv.com/content/product-market-fit-survey); [Umbrex framework page](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/product-management-frameworks/sean-ellis-product-market-fit-survey/); [Builtin, which PMF metric](https://builtin.com/articles/product-market-fit)
- Critique: the 40% figure is a correlation Ellis observed in his late-2000s portfolio, not a causal threshold. Some 2026 commentary argues pre-PMF teams should use other methods. [opinion] — [Rework, Sean Ellis test](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/startup-fundamentals/sean-ellis-test); [getperspective.ai, "PMF survey is dead"](https://getperspective.ai/blog/pmf-survey-is-dead-2026-what-pre-pmf-teams-run-instead)
- **Superhuman PMF engine** (Rahul Vohra, First Round Review, 2018). It uses the Ellis question, with ≥40% "very disappointed" as the marker of early PMF. Next, *segment* to find supporters and draw a "high-expectation customer" profile. Then analyse what supporters love and what holds back the "somewhat disappointed". Then build the roadmap from both. Superhuman's score reportedly went from 22% to 58% in under a year. [the score trajectory and step count (4 vs. 5) vary between secondary sources] — [Superhuman blog (original essay)](https://blog.superhuman.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); [Business of Software talk, Vohra 2018](https://businessofsoftware.org/2018/11/product-market-fit-engine-rahul-vohra-ceo-superhuman/); [David Cummings summary](https://davidcummings.org/2020/10/10/running-the-product-market-fit-engine-the-superhuman-way/); [Product School podcast page](https://productschool.com/resources/product-podcast/rahul-vohra-superhuman-pmf-engine-ai-native)
- **JTBD / switch interviews.** One JTBD write-up of Slack argues that "chat is better than email" was never the real job. Its point: the reason people switch is often not the feature the team believes in. [opinion] — [JTBD UX protocol, "The Slack equation"](https://jtbdux-protocol.pages.dev/foundations/jtbd-101/the-slack-equation-why-chat-is-better-than-email-was-never-the-point)

### Inferences
- For a pre-launch or early app with few users, steps 1 and 3 (hypothesis plus experiment) and qualitative interviews matter more than regression, because correlation analysis needs enough retained and churned users in each cohort to mean anything.
- The Ellis/Superhuman survey suits a subscription app well: survey active subscribers after they have used the product (Superhuman surveyed users who had used it recently and more than a couple of times). Then segment by persona to find who the magic moment works for.

### Gaps
- The "smile curve" (a retention curve that flattens and then rises as users return) and Amplitude "Compass" / Mixpanel "Signal" / impact analysis: no primary vendor documentation could be fetched (amplitude.com blocked). These are known features, but they are not cited here.
- Bob Moesta / Clayton Christensen switch-interview method (forces of progress: push, pull, anxiety, habit): not sourced in this pass.

## Designing toward the magic moment

### Takeaway
Behaviour models agree: cut the steps to the aha, give a prompt when the user is able and motivated, and get the user to invest something that makes the next visit more valuable. Subscription data argues strongly for showing value in the *first session*, because most trials start on install day. Habit research says habits take weeks to months, vary hugely between people, and survive a missed day. Behavioural effects (endowed progress, IKEA) are real but have boundary conditions.

### Cited Findings
- **Fogg Behavior Model, B = MAP.** Behaviour happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt come together at the same moment. If a behaviour doesn't happen, at least one is missing. There are three prompt types: Spark (low motivation), Facilitator (low ability), Signal (both high). It was originally written B=MAT ("trigger"), and renamed in *Tiny Habits* (2019). [documented: Fogg's own site] — [behaviormodel.org](https://behaviormodel.org/); [The Behavioral Scientist on B=MAP](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=3058)
- **Hook model** (Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, 2014): trigger → action → variable reward → investment. The investment phase stores value and "loads the next trigger". Variable reward sets it apart from a plain feedback loop. [secondary summaries of the book] — [Farnam Street on Hooked](https://fs.blog/hooked/); [Google Play Dev: Optimize app retention with the Hooked model](https://medium.com/googleplaydev/optimize-app-retention-with-the-hooked-model-a0781f8e5d29); [strategy+business critique, "Zombify your customers"](https://strategy-business.com/blog/Zombify-Your-Customers)
- **Habit formation, Lally et al.** ("How are habits formed", *European Journal of Social Psychology*, DOI 10.1002/ejsp.674, online 2009, print 2010). 96 participants repeated a daily health behaviour for 84 days. Median/mean time to peak automaticity was about 66 days, ranging from 18 days to a *modelled* 254 days. About half never reached a habit-level score in 84 days. Missing one day did not significantly harm habit formation, but very inconsistent people failed. [documented peer-reviewed, via UCL and BPS coverage] — [UCL news, 2009](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)
- Critique: the 66-day average comes from the 39 participants whose curves fit well, and the upper bound is a projection, not an observation. [commentary] — [The Behavioral Scientist, "What Lally et al. actually found"](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit)
- **Endowed progress** (Nunes & Drèze, 2006). At a car wash, 300 loyalty cards were handed out. One version needed 8 stamps. The other needed 10 stamps with 2 already filled in. The pre-filled card produced more completions. The effect depends on why the head start is given and on the currency progress is shown in. [documented field experiment, via summaries] — [Coglode, Endowed progress effect](https://www.coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect); [Medium UX write-up](https://medium.com/davidteodorescu/design-perfect-ux-tasks-the-endowed-progress-effect-7461ca20076c)
- **IKEA effect** (Norton, Mochon & Ariely, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 2012): people overvalue things they built themselves. The effect disappears if they don't finish or if they destroy what they built. [documented] — [Harvard DASH working paper](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-2473-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content); [BehavioralEconomics.com](https://www.behavioraleconomics.com/mini-encyclopedia-of-be/ikea-effect/)
- **Paywall timing, subscription apps** (RevenueCat State of Subscription Apps 2025). About 80–82% of trial starts happen on install day (Day 0). The median app converts about 1.9% of downloads to paid within 35 days, and top apps about 8.5%. Hard paywalls convert about 5x better than freemium, with a median D35 of roughly 12.1% vs. 2.2% (2025). The 2026 edition reports roughly 10.7% vs. 2.1%, with about 89% of trials starting on Day 0. More than 55% of 3-day trials are cancelled on Day 0, against 31% of 7-day trials. [vendor data; the figures mix 2025 and 2026 editions in secondary coverage] — [RevenueCat State of Subscription Apps 2025](https://revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2025); [Airbridge, paywall conversion](https://www.airbridge.io/en/blog/paywall-conversion-structural-decisions); [Airbridge, installs but no subscribers](https://www.airbridge.io/en/blog/why-subscription-app-get-installs-no-paid-subscribers); [SaaStr top 10 learnings](https://saastr.com/the-top-10-learnings-from-revenuecats-state-of-subscription-apps-how-115000-mobile-apps-deliver-16b-in-revenue-whats-working-whats-quietly-killing-growth)
- Elman (Twitter) reported that *adding* steps to sign-up (to collect interests and suggest follows) still raised outcomes, cited as +29% sign-ups. The lesson is that steps which speed up the aha are worth adding. [secondary, weak] — [Medium, Reading Josh Elman on growth](https://medium.com/growth-hacker/reading-josh-elman-on-growth-6d379a6c153d)

### Inferences
- "Show value before asking" and "paywall on Day 0" pull against each other. The resolution the data suggests: put a taste of the magic moment *inside the first session, before or alongside* the paywall, because almost all trial decisions happen on Day 0. A 3-day trial leaves very little time for a habit to form (Lally's median is about 66 days), so the trial has to sell the aha and the *promise* of the habit, not the habit itself.
- Investment (Hook) and the IKEA effect both favour onboarding where the user *creates or saves something of their own* (preferences, a first plan, saved items). Endowed progress favours showing a setup checklist that is already partly done.
- Streak design should forgive a single miss (Lally: one missed day doesn't break a habit; Duolingo: streak freezes and streak saver).

### Gaps
- **Peak-end rule** (Kahneman et al., 1993): not sourced in this pass. The standard claim is that experiences are remembered by their peak and their ending, not their average.
- "Reducing steps to the aha" benchmark data (e.g. activation lift per removed step) were not found.

## Value proposition, positioning and "selling point" frameworks

### Takeaway
April Dunford's positioning order is: competitive alternatives → unique attributes → value → target customers → market category, in that order, because it follows how customers think. The magic moment should be the moment a user *feels* the differentiated value named in that chain.

### Cited Findings
- Dunford's five components: competitive alternatives (what customers would do if you didn't exist), unique attributes (capabilities you have and alternatives lack), (differentiated) value, target customers ("best-fit" segments), market category. She starts from alternatives "because that's how customers think." The book mentions a "+1" layer (relevant trends), but this was not confirmed in the results. [secondary summaries] — [Heinz Marketing summary pt. 2](https://www.heinzmarketing.com/blog/five-components-of-effective-positioning-an-obviously-awesome-book-summary-part-2/); [Antoine Buteau, lessons from April Dunford](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-april-dunford.md); [Miro template](https://miro.com/miroverse/april-dunfords-positioning-template)

### Inferences
- For a recipe/meal-planning app, the competitive alternatives are not other apps alone. They also include "free recipe sites/TikTok + a notes app", "a paper list", and "the same five dinners on rotation". The magic moment has to beat *those*, and fast.

### Gaps
- Strategyzer Value Proposition Canvas (customer jobs / pains / gains ↔ products / pain relievers / gain creators): not sourced in this pass.
- "Hair on fire" problem test (often credited to Michael Seibel / YC) and "painkiller vs. vitamin": no primary source fetched. Treat the attributions as [folklore] until checked.
- One-sentence promise formats (e.g. Geoffrey Moore's positioning statement template): not sourced.

## Pitfalls: vanity aha, correlation ≠ causation, paywalls, AI novelty decay

### Takeaway
The biggest risks: treating a correlated action as causal, picking an aha that is easy to trigger but doesn't predict retention, and, for AI features, reading early retention while "AI tourists" are still in the cohort. AI subscription apps show weaker annual retention than non-AI apps, so a novelty-driven magic moment is not enough to keep subscribers.

### Cited Findings
- Facebook's "7 friends" was a correlation and a *proxy* for the felt aha (the social graph coming alive), not a guaranteed cause of retention. [secondary] — [PM case study](https://scratch-puffin-363.notion.site/Facebook-s-7-friends-activation-story-a-PM-case-study-33dec508b5118053bff8e0233fedd0f3)
- Lenny's method makes step 3 an experiment "to prove causation." [secondary] — [Personal Learnings mirror](https://personal-learnings.pages.dev/reading_room/issues/lennys_newsletter/0440_how-to-determine-your-activation-metric.html)
- Duolingo's first gamification attempt did not move retention, which shows that plausible "engagement" mechanics can be vanity. [documented, Mazal] — [Lenny's Newsletter, Duolingo](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)
- **AI novelty decay.** After looking at hundreds of AI companies, a16z found that early retention curves are distorted by "AI tourists" who churn in the first months. It recommends rebasing retention from Month 0 to Month 3 and judging durability by Month 12. [documented, a16z] — [a16z, AI retention benchmarks](https://a16z.com/ai-retention-benchmarks/); [a16z, "Cinderella glass slipper effect"](https://a16z.com/the-cinderella-glass-slipper-effect-retention-rules-in-the-ai-era/); [a16z, 7 ways AI-native companies can improve retention](https://a16z.com/7-ways-ai-native-companies-can-improve-retention/)
- RevenueCat 2026 data, as cited secondhand: AI apps keep about 21.1% of annual subscribers against 30.7% for non-AI apps, and about 6.1% vs. 9.5% on monthly plans. [secondary; verify in report] — [DEV Community summary](https://dev.to/zaraai_0b75675ddc9204c716/ai-apps-generate-41-more-revenue-per-user-but-lose-79-of-annual-subscribers-before-month-13-485a); [Rework, why AI products churn](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/ai-startup-growth/ai-product-churn)
- Hard paywalls convert better than freemium, but early trial cancellations are heavy (55%+ of 3-day trials cancel on Day 0). A magic moment that only happens on Day 3 or later can miss the decision window. [vendor data via secondary] — [Airbridge](https://www.airbridge.io/en/blog/paywall-conversion-structural-decisions)
- Ethics critique of habit design ("zombify your customers"). [opinion] — [strategy+business](https://strategy-business.com/blog/Zombify-Your-Customers)

### Inferences
- For an AI feature, define the magic moment as a *useful outcome the user keeps* (a plan they cook from, a list they shop with), not as "AI generated something impressive". Measure retention of the AI feature rebased to Month 3.
- Do not lift another app's threshold ("7 friends", "30 follows") as your own target. Each was found in that company's own data.

### Gaps
- No rigorous study was found on whether a magic moment experienced *before* a hard paywall raises trial-to-paid conversion compared with one experienced after it. RevenueCat's Day-0 data is consistent with "show value early", but it does not isolate that effect.
- Specific vanity-aha case studies (an aha that was easy to trigger but didn't predict retention) were not found, apart from Duolingo's failed gamification.
